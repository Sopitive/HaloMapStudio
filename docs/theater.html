Theater

Halo Map Studio opens Halo: Reach saved films and plays them back on the map they were recorded on — the players, the vehicles and the Forge scene they were fighting in. It also reads the gametype the match was played under, and can pull per-player statistics out of a film with no window and no graphics card at all.

Reach only. Halo 3, Halo 4 and Halo 2 Anniversary films are not read. A clip from another title is reported as such when you open it rather than failing silently.

Opening a film

File ▸ Open saved film… opens a clip, and films you have opened before are listed in File ▸ Recent alongside maps and variants. The browser starts in the folders MCC actually writes films to, so in most installations you do not have to go looking.

Three kinds of file are read:

ExtensionWhat it is
.movA saved film. This is what MCC writes when you save a clip from Theater.
.filmThe same container under a different name, used by some MCC builds.
.tempA recording still in progress — the film of the match you are playing right now. See below.

Where films live

On Windows, under your MCC local data:

%LOCALAPPDATA%Low\MCC\Temporary\UserContent\HaloReach\Movie\
%LOCALAPPDATA%Low\MCC\LocalFiles\<account>\HaloReach\Movie\

On Linux, MCC runs under Proton, so the same folders sit inside the game's prefix:

~/.steam/steam/steamapps/compatdata/976730/pfx/drive_c/users/steamuser/
    AppData/LocalLow/MCC/Temporary/UserContent/HaloReach/Movie/

HMS also scans mounted drives (/mnt, /media, /run/media) for MCC data directories, so films on a Windows partition you still have mounted are found without being told where they are. File ▸ Open saved film… lists every folder it found, with a film count beside each.

Watching a match you are still recording

A .temp file is the film MCC is writing as you play. HMS can open one and play back everything recorded so far, so a good moment can be re-watched without leaving the match first. Two things differ from a finished film:

  • The length written in the header is a placeholder, so HMS measures the clip from the data that is actually there rather than trusting it.
  • An in-progress film has no author record, so player names and armour are bound by a different route (the recording player is identified by the live aim the file carries). It works, but on some clips fewer players are named than in the finished version of the same film.

Playing it back

The transport sits in a fixed strip under the viewport: play / pause, step a frame at a time, a scrubber, the clock, a playback speed, and the number of players alive at the current moment. The strip never changes height, so the play button does not move under the cursor while a clip runs.

The cast and detail panels live in a side panel that is closed by default, and the Forge editing column folds away while a clip is open (the Editor toggle brings it back), so the picture gets essentially the whole window.

The map the film was recorded on is loaded automatically when it can be found, and the map variant is read out of the film itself — a saved film embeds the whole Forge variant, so the arena you are watching is the one that was actually played, not the stock map.

Cameras: free, follow, first person

CameraBehaviour
FreeThe ordinary HMS camera (see Camera & navigation) — fly anywhere while the clip plays.
FollowSits behind and slightly above a chosen player and tracks them. The mouse orbits around them at a fixed distance; recenter puts the camera back behind them.
First personPuts the camera at that player's eye, looking along their own recorded aim, and draws Reach's dedicated first-person arm model holding their weapon. The player you are watching through is not drawn, so you are not looking at the inside of their helmet.

The first-person arms are posed by the first-person animation of the weapon the player is carrying. Where a weapon gives the arms no pose to play, the arms are not drawn and the panel says so rather than showing a frozen pose.

Who is who

The cast list names every player in the match, with their service tag and team colour, and gives each one a follow and a 1st person button. Floating name tags above the players use the same team colours.

Bodies are drawn with the armour each player was actually wearing — helmet, shoulders, chest, wrist, utility, knees and the armour effects, with their own primary and secondary colours — read out of the film's own player records.

Players are matched to bodies through the owner stamp the recording puts on each body. On a clip where that stamp cannot be resolved for someone, HMS says "no biped could be matched to that player in this film" instead of guessing or silently doing nothing.

The gametype it was played under

A saved film embeds the entire game variant, and HMS decodes its settings, not just its name: the loadout palettes (name, primary and secondary weapon, armour ability and grenades for all thirty loadouts), the base player traits, the respawn and round settings, the social and map settings, the eight team configurations and the player species rule. They are listed on the Theater card under the gametype's title.

This is also where the weapons in players' hands come from. A recording only names the weapon a player is holding when it changes, so for most of a match nobody's weapon is ever mentioned. The gametype states what everyone spawned with, so bodies are given that weapon from the moment they appear, and a real pickup takes over from the tick the recording reports it.

Weapons are drawn at the size the game draws them, which is not one size: Reach authors a separate scale for a weapon lying on the ground and for a weapon in the hands of each size of character, so an energy sword is smaller in a Spartan's hands than it is on the floor, and larger in an Elite's.

Where the gametype cannot answer — its selectable loadouts disagree and a film does not record which loadout a player chose, or it leaves the weapon to the map's own default — the card says exactly that. It does not invent a weapon, and it does not report a setting that says nothing as though it were a measurement.

Statistics, in the app and on the command line

The Player statistics section of the side panel gives one row per player: score, lives, deaths, time alive, longest life, distance travelled, top speed on foot, time sprinting, airborne and standing still, time in a vehicle and which one, and the weapons the recording named.

The same numbers come out of the command line with no window and no GPU, so they work over SSH, in a script, or while the app is already running:

hms-app --film-stats <film or folder> [--json] [--map <cache.map>]
ArgumentMeaning
<film or folder>One film, or a folder — every film in it is processed, a file that cannot be read is reported and the rest carry on. Several paths may be given.
--jsonMachine-readable output instead of the table.
--map <cache.map>Optional. Only used to turn vehicle, weapon and loadout ids into names.

Every figure in the JSON carries a provenance label — decoded (read from the file), derived (computed from what was read, with the rule stated) or not available — so a consumer can filter on confidence without reading this page.

Scripting

The same operations are available to the script host and the command server. Pulling data out of a recording (objects, zones, events, Megalo variables, score) has its own page, Film data:

film <path>              open a clip
film info | scene | index what was read: header, map variant, decoded stream
film play | pause
film seek <seconds>      SECONDS, not ticks
film step <n>            n frames (negative steps back)
film speed <x>           playback rate
film camera free | follow <slot> | first <slot>
film stats [json]        the same statistics as --film-stats
film extract variant|gametype [dir]   write the embedded map variant (.mvar) / game variant (.bin) out
film folders | records   where films were found; raw record listing
film variant | megalo | events | seats | score | objects | object | inside | near | dump
                         the data verbs: CSV / JSON out of the recording (see Film data)

The data verbs (variant, megalo, events, seats, score, objects, object, inside, near, dump) print CSV with a header row, or JSON when json is given. Their filters, columns, time syntax and examples are on the Film data page. Most of them need film index first; film megalo and film variant read the file themselves.

Playback draws the film’s own Forge scene automatically. The first film index, film seek or film play after a film is opened rebuilds the map the recording was made on, from the variant the film carries, and reports it next to the actor count — Forge scene: 452 object(s) from "Xenon // Castle". If it cannot, it says so in the same line and why: the film was recorded on a stock map, or HMS has no saved .mvar for that map to use as a canvas. film scene forces a redraw.

Opening a film with film <path> reads the header only and needs no map loaded, so film info, film stats, film records and film extract all work on a machine that has no maps installed at all.

film extract variant is worth knowing about on its own: it writes the Forge map variant embedded in the film out as a .mvar, so a map you only have a clip of can be opened in the editor.

film extract gametype writes the game variant the film was recorded with out as a real Halo: Reach .bin gametype file, named from its title, plus a small .txt of notes beside it. A film embeds the gametype whole as the game’s in-memory struct; Halo Map Studio re-packs it into the file format the game saves (a BLF container around the bit-packed mpvr chunk) with an encoder that matches the game’s own serialiser bit for bit on the several hundred real gametypes it was checked against, Megalo scripts, string tables and Firefight variants included. The file is rebuilt from the film’s own variant — it is never copied from a similarly named file on disk, because MCC keeps the same lineage id on every derived save. To use it, drop it into the game_variants folder next to your saved gametypes. HMS has not loaded it in MCC itself; if the game refuses a file, please report it. If a variant cannot be encoded (a string table too large for a .bin), a raw .reach_gv dump of the struct is written instead and the notes say why.

Megalo variables in a recording

A film embeds the whole game variant, so it knows which Megalo variables the gametype declares, and it records the values the game chose to send (global, player and team variables with network priority low or high, plus the round clock and game state), each as a time series. Variables with priority none or local, per-object variables and variable names are not in a film. film megalo reads all of it; see Film data ▸ film megalo for the forms, filters and how to read timers and object references.

Objects, labels and zones in a recording

Every object is an entity in the recording, with its type, lifetime, position, parent and (for player bodies) owner; the stream also states each object’s team and boundary shape (sphere, cylinder or box). The Forge label, spawn sequence and respawn time are not in the stream: HMS takes them from the map variant the film embeds, matching each object to its placement by position (join_dist reports the match distance). Positions are sparse samples held until the next one, so a fast player can cross a small zone between two samples, and box zones are approximate. film objects, film object and film inside / film near read all of this; see Film data.

Highlighting script objects

Open the Theater side panel and tick Highlight script objects (off by default; the choice and the category filter are remembered). Objects the gametype's script is responsible for are outlined in a colour per category, with a small label (name, id, team) over the nearest ones, and listed in the panel. Click a row to move the camera to the object; click the same row again to follow it (click once more to stop).

CategoryWhat the recording says
objective / flagthe flag, ball, bomb, skull ... (the Theater object lane's objective list)
attached markera generic object that rides something and is a marker: it states a zone shape or non-default properties, has no model, or a variable refers to it (the race clip's hill markers glued to players)
zone / hillit states a boundary shape (sphere, cylinder or box), replicated or from its Forge placement
script-spawneda generic object the map variant never placed, with a team, flags or a shape
labelled Forgea placed object with a Forge label or spawn sequence
script variableany other object a replicated Megalo variable refers to
invisible / no modelnone of the above, but its definition draws nothing (4 or fewer render triangles, or an invisible_* name)

Bodies and projectiles are never highlighted; the entities that carry the player, team and global variables are counted in the panel but not drawn. An object with no drawable model (an invisible_cube_of_derek, a hill-marker zone, a door that only draws an effect) gets a translucent placeholder of its real extents: its zone shape, else the oriented bounds of its collision or physics hull, else a small cube. With the highlight off nothing is drawn for them, so the default view matches the game. film highlight [csv|json] lists the same objects for scripts. Not applied: a scripted object scale, which a recording does not state.

What a recording actually contains

A Reach saved film is not a video. It is a recording of the game's network traffic — the same stream the other players' consoles were sent — replayed against the same map and the same gametype. That shapes what HMS can and cannot tell you, and HMS is explicit about the difference everywhere it matters:

  • Positions are sparse. A body's position is only transmitted on some ticks; in between, every machine integrates velocity. Distance travelled is therefore a lower bound, and it is labelled as one.
  • Movement is reconstructed. Which animation a player was playing is not something the stream states for every body on every tick, so a player's gait — idle, walking, running, sprinting, airborne — is derived from how they actually moved and which way they were facing.
  • Some things the stream does state outright, and those are read rather than guessed: vehicle entry and exit with the seat index, scores, and which character model each body was spawned as.
  • The kill feed is one player's, not the match's. A film is a recording of what one machine was sent, and in Reach the medal and kill feed is personal, so the recording names the kills of the player who saved the clip and nobody else's. On a measured example: 163 bodies destroyed, 16 kill rows, every one naming the same player — the recorder. Where the recording states a kill, HMS says so and can name the killer, the victim and the tick; for every other death it has the death but not the story. The damage record names a responsible unit for many of the rest, and where that is used it is labelled as derived rather than stated.
  • Some things are simply not in the file. Which loadout a player chose is player-side state and was never transmitted. A weapon the gametype leaves to "the map's default" is resolved from a tag the film does not carry. Where that is the case, HMS says so instead of filling the gap.

The general rule this feature is built on: a number that was read from the file and a number that was worked out from it are different kinds of fact, and the one that was worked out always says so.
