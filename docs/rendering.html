Rendering & settings

The settings window has two collapsible sections, Lighting and Render & display, plus an Advanced (diagnostics) section of read-only readouts (camera pose, map, a "rebuild forge objects" button) that you do not need for forging. Settings are not saved between runs.

Lighting

By default the app reproduces the game's own exposure: the map's camera-effects tag provides the auto-exposure key and the range of EV it may adapt over, and the luminance meter converges the way the engine's does. The sliders let you deviate from that for inspection.

Real-time probe GI and the path-traced bake

Both are optional alternatives to the map's baked lightmaps, mainly useful to preview lighting for objects or to see a map under a different sun.

  • Real-time lighting (probe GI) keeps a grid of light probes updated on the GPU every frame (a DDGI-style probe grid with visibility). When on you get a GI gain slider (0.25–4, default 1.3), sun yaw / pitch / intensity sliders seeded from the map with reset sun to map, and — if Lights is shown — a move light picker that lets you drag any of the 24 nearest map lights (or move one to the camera). The info line reports the probe count, spacing and update rate. Outdoors it matches the shipped bake closely; interiors are darker than the bake.
  • Path-traced lighting ignores the map's lightmaps and bakes a GPU path-traced global illumination of the level (a few seconds, progress bar shown). bake quality offers direct_only, draft, low, medium, high and super_slow (increasing samples and bounces). Untick to return to the stock lighting.

How accurate is the renderer?

The renderer's goal is to show a map the way the game shows it, by decoding the same data the engine uses rather than approximating the look:

  • Lightmaps — the per-map baked lighting (both the texture atlas and the per-vertex lighting used by terrain and instances) is decoded with the engine's own formulas, including the map's absolute intensity scale, so different maps are correctly bright or dark relative to each other.
  • Exposure and tone-mapping — auto-exposure uses each map's authored key and adaptation range, and the final tone curve matches the PC engine (no filmic curve). Per-map bloom and colour filters are read from the map's camera-effects tag.
  • Materials — terrain (multi-layer detail blending), opaque/detail shaders, glass, self-illumination, water (wave animation, reflections, foam and murk), the sky, foliage and decals follow the engine's shader templates, with textures decoded from the map's bitmaps (all common DXT / BC formats, including the DXN normal maps).
  • Effects — effect particle systems (grav lifts, waterfalls, sparks, mist) are simulated on the CPU from the map's effect tags; shield doors, holograms and light volumes use the same parameters as the game.
  • Objects — Forge pieces and scenario objects are lit the way the engine lights objects (sampling the lightmap under the object plus an air-probe term and a bounce term) so a crate in a shaded corner is darker than one in the sun.
  • Sun and shadows — the sun direction comes from the map; the sun shadow map covers objects only, since the level's own sun shadowing is already baked into the lightmap, exactly as in the game.
  • Fog — atmospheric fog uses the engine's scattering model and the map's fog parameters.

Much of this has been checked pixel-for-pixel against frames captured from the game with the same camera. Known remaining differences (v0.1.0): some indoor mid-tones render darker than the game (the engine's wide bloom "pedestal" is only partially reproduced), instanced geometry lit by per-vertex lighting can read too dark on some maps, a few water hues and the Forge World tide pools are slightly off, and a handful of exotic shader templates fall back to a neutral material. None of this affects what is written to a variant — rendering is for viewing only.
